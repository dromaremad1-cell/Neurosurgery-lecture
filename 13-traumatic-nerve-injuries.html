Complete Theory

Source Content Etiology

Cutting injuries are the most common cause, usually sharp instruments. Other causes: traction, electrical trauma (may extend to anterior horn cell), ischemic damage from severe/prolonged compression such as plaster cast, gunshot laceration, and chemical injury from acidic or alkaline agents.

Source Content Seddon Classification — Neuropraxia

Transient physiological dysfunction, often blunt injury; motor function more affected than sensory/autonomic; source states recovery in 24–48 hours.

Source Content Seddon Classification — Axonotmesis

Epineurium and perineurium intact; endoneurial axons affected. Complete motor/sensory/autonomic paralysis and loss of distal conductivity. May follow blunt or nearby penetrating trauma. Spontaneous regeneration may occur with good prognosis, though fascicular disruption can prevent regeneration despite gross continuity.

Source Content Seddon Classification — Neurotmesis

Most severe injury with complete nerve division, complete motor/sensory/autonomic paralysis, distal loss of conductivity and Wallerian degeneration. No regeneration without operative repair. Occurs in cuts, gunshots and long-bone fractures.

Source Content Brachial Plexus & Shoulder Injuries

Complete brachial-plexus injury → flaccid paralysis in severe shoulder injury. Upper-plexus Erb's Duchenne palsy (C5,C6) affects deltoid, supra/infraspinatus, biceps and brachialis, producing waiter's/policeman's-tip deformity. Lower-plexus Klumpke palsy (C7,8 in source) affects small hand muscles and finger flexors and may be associated with Horner syndrome. Long thoracic nerve injury → winged scapula.

Source Content Axilla Injuries

Musculocutaneous: biceps/brachialis weakness + lateral forearm sensory deficit. Radial: weakness of elbow/wrist/finger extensors → wrist/finger drop, dorsal-hand/base-of-thumb sensory loss, lost triceps reflex. Median: finger-flexor and thumb-opposition weakness, ape hand, lateral palm + lateral 3½-finger sensory loss. Ulnar: weak little-finger flexion, interossei weakness, weak finger ab/adduction and thumb adduction, partial claw hand, medial hand + medial 1½-finger sensory loss.

Source Content Arm Injuries — Radial & Median

Radial in arm: wrist drop with preserved elbow extensors and dorsolateral-hand sensory loss. Median cut at/below elbow: pointing index deformity, ape hand from thenar atrophy, ulnar deviation, weak radial grip, weak pronation, failed thumb opposition; pencil test for abductor pollicis brevis; lateral 3½-finger sensory loss, sometimes effectively 2½ due to ulnar overlap.

Source Content Ulnar Nerve Injury

Partial claw hand, radial deviation from FCU paralysis, sunken interosseous spaces, hypothenar/medial forearm wasting. Motor tests: weak ulnar grip, failed finger ab/adduction, cardboard test, Froment's sign/paper test, failed little-finger abduction. Ulnar paradox: higher lesion → less clawing because FDP is also paralyzed.

Source sensory-level detail: lesion above 5 cm from the wrist → palmar and dorsal surfaces of the medial 1½ fingers plus medial third of palm; lesion below 5 cm from the wrist → only palmar surface affected because the dorsal branch arises above that level; lesion below the wrist → source states no sensory changes because the superficial branch escapes.

Source Content Forearm/Wrist/Hand Injuries

Median injury: thenar + first/second lumbrical paralysis, ape hand, thenar atrophy, pencil test, lateral 3½-finger sensory loss with nail beds/lateral half palm, vasomotor/sudomotor changes.

Anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) syndrome: the source describes AIN as a pure motor branch of the median nerve. It innervates flexor pollicis longus (FPL), pronator quadratus (PQ), and the radial half of flexor digitorum profundus (FDP). Patients cannot make the normal “O/OK” sign; the pinch-grip test becomes abnormal, with no sensory affection. The source also gives practical examples such as difficulty buttoning a shirt or turning a car key.

Posterior interosseous nerve: pure motor; injury produces finger drop only and is listed as having the best prognosis after repair.

Source Content Lower Limb Nerve Injuries

Femoral: quadriceps/knee-extension weakness, lost knee jerk. Sciatic: may follow faulty IM injection/hip dislocation; foot drop. Common peroneal: fibular-neck trauma/cast compression; dorsiflexor/evertor weakness → foot drop; dorsum-foot sensory loss. Tibial: plantar-flexor weakness and weak ankle jerk.

Source Content Investigations

Nerve conduction, EMG, SSEP, MEP, plain X-ray, CT and MRI.

Source Content Treatment & Nerve Repair

Neuropraxia/axonotmesis: source states regeneration is possible and operative repair is not required. Neurotmesis: indication for nerve repair. Methods: primary nerve suture for clean cuts immediately; secondary suture for crushed/contaminated wounds after 3–6 weeks; nerve transposition, graft (sural or median antecubital cutaneous donor in source), nerve anastomosis and neurolysis.

Source Content Outcome Factors

Better in children; delay >3 months worsens prognosis; distance from injury to terminal branches matters. The source gives regeneration about 1–2 mm/day and separately states a maximum rate after 3 weeks of about 1 mm/day. Pure motor or sensory nerves do better than mixed nerves; the injured extremity should be cared for and joints kept in functional position; postoperative physical therapy is needed.

High-Yield Summary

Seddon classification

Neuropraxia = transient block; axonotmesis = axonal injury with preserved outer sheaths; neurotmesis = complete division requiring repair.

Upper limb

Erb/ Klumpke plexus lesions; radial = wrist drop; median = ape hand/pointing index; ulnar = claw hand/Froment; long thoracic = winged scapula.

Lower limb

Femoral = weak knee extension/lost knee jerk; common peroneal = foot drop; tibial = weak plantar flexion/ankle jerk.

Investigations

NCS, EMG, SSEP, MEP, X-ray, CT, MRI.

Treatment

Neuropraxia/axonotmesis usually regenerate; neurotmesis needs operative repair.

5 Minutes Before Exam

Neuropraxia: Transient block; motor > sensory; recovery 24–48 h in source.
Axonotmesis: Axonal disruption with epineurium/perineurium intact; spontaneous regeneration may occur.
Neurotmesis: Complete division + Wallerian degeneration; no recovery without repair.
Erb: C5-C6 upper plexus → waiter's-tip deformity.
Klumpke: Lower plexus → intrinsic hand weakness ± Horner syndrome.
Radial: Wrist drop.
Median: Ape hand / pointing index / failed opposition.
Ulnar: Partial claw hand + Froment + interosseous wasting.
Common peroneal: Fibular-neck injury → foot drop.
Repair: Primary suture clean cut; secondary 3–6 weeks if crushed/contaminated.
