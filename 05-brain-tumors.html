Complete Theory

Etiology
  • Genetic-induced: meningioma, neurofibroma and tumor-related gene abnormalities.
  • Virus-induced: DNA tumor viruses (papovavirus, adenovirus) and RNA retrovirus/oncorna group.
  • Radiation-induced: fibrosarcoma, meningioma, glioma.
  • Chemically-induced: carcinogens.
Classification
  • Biological behavior: benign, malignant, local-extension tumors. Malignant examples in the source include glioblastoma multiforme (listed as the most common primary intra-axial malignant tumor in adults), neuroblastoma, hemangioblastoma, medulloblastoma and pineoblastoma.
  • Tissue of origin: primary (bone, meninges, brain/glial/neuronal/pineal, cranial nerve, pituitary) and secondary (orbit, nasopharynx, paranasal sinus, neck, metastases).
  • Cell of origin: differentiated, poorly differentiated, undifferentiated.
  • Age: adult—meningioma, schwannoma, pituitary adenoma; pediatric—medulloblastoma, ependymoma, craniopharyngioma.
Raised intracranial pressure
  • Intermittent dull early-morning headache due to nocturnal hypercapnia/recumbency.
  • Worsened by cough or abdominal straining.
  • Projectile vomiting.
  • Papilledema.
  • Diplopia with VI involvement.
  • Dysequilibrium/vertigo.
  • VI palsy from stretch/compression near Dorello's canal.
Focal neurological deficits
  • Non-dominant frontal: contralateral UMN hemiparesis, sphincter incontinence, attention deficit, depression, social indifference, Foster-Kennedy syndrome.
  • Dominant frontal: same + motor aphasia.
  • Dominant parietal: sensory threshold increase, primary sensory loss, contralateral agnosia, inferior quadrantanopia, sensory aphasia.
  • Non-dominant parietal: similar but contralateral apraxias.
  • Temporal: auditory agnosia, superior quadrantanopia, memory loss, complex seizures; dominant adds sensory dysphasia/verbal memory loss.
  • Occipital: contralateral visuospatial neglect and homonymous hemianopia.
  • Pineal: Parinaud sign + obstructive hydrocephalus.
  • Cerebellar: ataxia, hypotonia, dyskinesia.
  • Brainstem: ipsilateral CN deficit + contralateral hemiparesis + contralateral superficial/deep sensory loss.
  • Pituitary: endocrine hyper/hyposecretion + bitemporal hemianopia + hypothalamic signs.
  • CPA: VII/VIII affection, tinnitus, cerebellar and brainstem signs, raised ICP.
Brain shift / herniation
  • Ipsilateral III palsy.
  • Contralateral hemiparesis.
  • Meningism.
  • Coma.
  • Apnea.
  • Cardiac arrest.
Tumor apoplexy
  • Listed with pituitary adenoma, meningioma and melanoma metastasis.
Investigations
  • Plain X-ray: abnormal calcification, bone erosion, sella widening.
  • CT: lesion, associated hemorrhage or calcifications.
  • MRI: gold standard.
  • Cerebral angiography: for vascular tumors such as hemangioblastoma.
Treatment
  • Surgery: stereotactic biopsy, endoscopic biopsy, transsphenoidal pituitary excision, craniotomy/excision, CSF diversion for hydrocephalus.
  • Radiation: fractionated external beam and radiosurgery.
  • Chemotherapy: systemic IV agents.
  • Hormonal therapy: bromocriptine/cabergoline for prolactinomas; replacement therapy for pituitary tumors.
  • Immune therapy, gene therapy, photoradiation therapy.
  • Drugs: levetiracetam for supratentorial tumors, corticosteroids for tumor edema, dehydrating measures for herniation/raised ICP.

High-Yield Summary

High-yield map

Etiology: genetic, viral, radiation, chemical. Classification: biological behavior, tissue/cell of origin, age. Clinical presentation: raised ICP, seizures, focal deficits, herniation, tumor apoplexy. MRI is the gold standard. Treatment includes surgery, radiation/radiosurgery, chemotherapy, hormonal/immune/gene/phototherapy, and supportive anti-edema/antiepileptic measures listed in the source.
