Complete Theory

Source Content Epidemiology & Etiology

More common in males and in young adults in the 2nd–4th decades. In old age, often secondary to osteoporosis or metastatic lesions. Source states 10% lead to paraplegia or quadriplegia and that spinal trauma is rare in children due to ligament laxity. Central cord syndrome in elderly may occur without fracture, often with pre-existing cervical stenosis.

Causes: road-traffic accidents (most common), falls from height, sports/diving/horse-riding/cycling, blunt trauma, heavy object falling on head.

Source Content Pathogenesis & Pathology

Mechanisms: direct compression by bone/ligament/disc, vascular interruption, traction injury, secondary hemorrhage. Injuries may involve vertebral body/lamina/spinous/transverse processes, joints/dislocations, ligaments, paravertebral muscles, discs and spinal cord.

Source Content Denis Three-Column Model

Anterior column: anterior half of vertebral body/disc and anterior longitudinal ligament. Middle: posterior half of body/disc and posterior longitudinal ligament. Posterior: posterior bony complex and posterior ligamentous complex. Source: one-column injury = stable; more than two columns disturbed = unstable.

Source Content Mechanisms of Spinal Injury

Flexion/compression → wedge fracture, usually stable. Hyperextension → spinous process/lamina/pedicle fractures. Flexion-rotation/translation → three-column fracture-dislocation, unstable and often neural injury. Vertical compression → burst fracture involving anterior + middle columns, highly unstable with marked neural injury. Distraction → severe ligamentous injury, unstable, e.g. Chance fracture. Lateral flexion → lateral wedge + ipsilateral pedicle fracture, usually stable.

Source Content Complete vs Incomplete Injury

Complete: total loss of motor, sensory and autonomic function below injury. The source states that about 3% of patients considered complete on initial examination may show some recovery within 24 hours; persistence beyond 24 hours is described as indicating no distal functional recovery, while later recovery is said to be very rare. Incomplete: any preserved motor or sensory function below injury; sacral sparing = preserved perianal sensation and voluntary rectal sphincter contraction.

Source Content Incomplete Cord Syndromes

Central cord, Brown-Séquard, anterior cord and posterior cord syndromes are listed. Preservation of sensation or voluntary movement below injury supports incomplete injury.

Source Content Clinical Picture

Neurological manifestations depend on level/site, adjacent-root involvement and speed of compression. Local spine signs: pain on movement, tenderness, muscle spasm, contusion of back/neck and deformity.

Source Content Radiological Investigations

Plain X-ray AP/lateral; dynamic neutral/flexion/extension study and whole-spine screening. CT/multislice CT is more accurate than MRI for fracture and displaced bone fragments. MRI shows cord contusion and cord insult/compression.

Source Content Resuscitation & Conservative Treatment

Immobilization with cervical collar if cervical fracture suspected and dorsolumbar brace for lower-spine fracture; ABC; careful neurological assessment. Conservative treatment in source: bed rest, analgesics, external support and methylprednisolone; source also stresses that medical treatment is not an alternative when surgery is indicated.

Source Content Surgical Treatment

Indications: unstable fracture even without neurological deficit, or stable fracture with neural compression. Aims: prevent further injury/complications, reduce displaced fracture (traction/manipulation), decompress neural structures from retropulsed bone, hematoma or disc fragments, and stabilize/fuse using posterior transpedicular screws/rods or anterior plate/screws with interbody cage fusion.

High-Yield Summary

Epidemiology

Young males most affected; RTA most common cause. Elderly injuries often relate to osteoporosis/metastases.

Instability

Denis three-column model: one column = stable; >2 columns disrupted = unstable.

Mechanisms

Flexion/compression, hyperextension, flexion-rotation, vertical compression/burst, distraction/Chance, lateral flexion.

Neurology

Complete = no motor/sensory/autonomic function below; incomplete = residual function, with sacral sparing.

Imaging

CT best for fracture/bone fragments; MRI for cord contusion/compression.

Treatment

Immobilize + ABC + neuro exam; surgery for unstable fracture or neural compression.

5 Minutes Before Exam

Most common cause: Road traffic accident.
Denis model: Anterior • middle • posterior columns.
Stable: One-column injury.
Unstable: >2 columns disturbed; burst/translation/distraction patterns often unstable.
Burst fracture: Vertical compression; anterior + middle columns; highly unstable.
Incomplete injury: Residual motor/sensory function below; sacral sparing is key.
CT: Best for fracture and displaced bone fragments.
MRI: Best for cord contusion/compression.
Surgery: Unstable fracture even without deficit, or stable fracture with neural compression.
