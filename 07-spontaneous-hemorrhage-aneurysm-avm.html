Complete Theory

Source Content Types & Etiology

Types: intracerebral/intraparenchymal, subarachnoid, intraventricular and subdural hemorrhage.

Etiologies: hypertension, AVM, aneurysmal rupture, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, intracranial neoplasm, coagulopathy, hemorrhagic transformation, cerebral venous thrombosis, sympathomimetic drug abuse, Moyamoya, sickle cell disease, eclampsia/postpartum vasculopathy, cerebritis and vasculitis.

Source Content ICH Pathogenesis

Arterial rupture into parenchyma abruptly increases ICP causing severe headache, impaired/lost consciousness and focal deficit. Perihematomal edema may cause shift/herniation. Small bleeds may mimic ischemic infarction until imaging. Deep hemorrhage suggests hypertension; superficial polar hemorrhages are associated with trauma in the source.

Source Content ICH Clinical Picture

Focal deficits. Distribution listed: putaminal 35%, lobar 25%, thalamic 20%, pontine 10%, cerebellar 10%. Also raised ICP, consciousness change, headache and neck stiffness when SAH is associated.

Source Content ICH Investigations

CBC/platelets, PT/aPTT. Imaging: CT, MRI, cerebral angiography.

Source Content ICH Medical & Surgical Treatment

Source medical management includes BP control; withholding anticoagulants and correcting INR with IV vitamin K + FFP; consideration of platelet transfusion for antiplatelet-related ICH; intermittent pneumatic compression, and later heparin after bleeding cessation in selected immobile patients; ventricular drainage for hydrocephalus; hypertonic saline or mannitol for ICP.

Surgical options listed: continuous ventricular drainage, craniotomy/evacuation, burr-hole aspiration, stereotactic thrombolysis/aspiration, endoscopic evacuation. Superficial sizable supratentorial hematoma may be evacuated in suitable patients; deep small hematoma is treated conservatively; cerebellar hematoma may require urgent posterior-fossa evacuation; pontine hematoma usually conservative; hydrocephalus may require ventricular drainage/VP shunt.

Source Content SAH Clinical Presentation

Sudden severe headache (classic feature), nausea/vomiting, meningeal irritation, photophobia/visual changes, focal deficits, sudden LOC and seizures.

Source Content SAH Complications

Rebleeding (listed as most common and most lethal), hydrocephalus, vasospasm (major cause of deterioration after clipping/coiling), seizures, cardiac dysfunction/arrhythmia and pulmonary edema.

Source Content SAH Investigations

Routine labs, leg-vein Doppler if DVT suspected, echo/ECG if cardiac complications, CT, lumbar puncture showing fresh blood early or xanthochromia later, MRA, CTA, and conventional/digital-subtraction angiography listed as gold standard.

Source Content SAH Treatment — Source Framing

Source medical treatment: immediate bed rest, sedation/analgesia, Triple-H therapy, oxygenation, corticosteroids, stool softener/H2 antagonist, antiepileptics, nimodipine for vasospasm prevention.

Surgical/endovascular approaches: clipping, trapping, wrapping, proximal ligation, and endovascular coil/balloon thrombosis especially for giant/complex aneurysms.

Source Content Intracranial Aneurysm

Source pathology: congenital defect in tunica media and elastic membrane at vessel bifurcation. Presentation commonly 40–60 years. Clinical picture may be asymptomatic, pressure symptoms, or leakage/rupture causing SAH.

Sites in the source: Anterior circulation 85% — anterior communicating artery (most common); internal carotid main trunk or junction with ophthalmic, posterior communicating, or anterior choroidal artery; anterior cerebral artery; middle cerebral artery. Posterior circulation 15% — basilar tip, superior cerebellar artery, PICA, AICA, and vertebral artery.

Pressure on the 3rd cranial nerve is emphasized particularly with posterior communicating artery aneurysm, producing ptosis or total ophthalmoplegia in the source.

Source Content AVM

Developmental anomaly with direct artery-to-vein communication due to absent capillary network; source calls it a common cause of hemorrhagic stroke in young adults. Types: AVM, arterial angioma, venous angioma, capillary telangiectasia. Presentation: asymptomatic, headache, hemorrhage (2–4% per year in the source), seizures, focal deficits. Investigations: CT, MRI, angiography, MRA, CTA, DSA gold standard. Treatment: observation in selected lesions, microsurgical excision, endovascular feeder embolization, stereotactic radiosurgery.

High-Yield Summary

ICH

Hypertension and other vascular/coagulopathic causes. Deep hemorrhage suggests HTN. CT/MRI/angiography; treat BP/ICP and reverse anticoagulation in source framing; surgery depends on site, size and clinical state.

SAH

Sudden severe headache + meningism ± LOC/seizures. Complications: rebleeding, hydrocephalus, vasospasm, seizures, cardiac dysfunction, pulmonary edema.

Imaging

CT, LP, MRA, CTA, DSA (gold standard in source).

Aneurysm / AVM

Aneurysm arises at bifurcation defect; AVM is direct artery-vein shunt without capillary bed. Treatment options include clipping/coiling and microsurgery/embolization/radiosurgery respectively.
